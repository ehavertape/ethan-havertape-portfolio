Section 01 · Business & Systems Analysis

University Pilot & Security Review

Before the University of Louisville would let all of its students use Due Gooder, the university had to be convinced the system was safe, private, and accessible. Getting there was a systems analysis project: identifying every stakeholder, translating their requirements into specific controls, documenting how our system met each one, and being honest about where it didn't yet.

Why this section uses Due Gooder work: I completed UofL's Sandbox program in place of CIS 320, 420, and 410, building Due Gooder full-time for course credit. This page applies the same methods those courses teach (systems analysis, project management, and case analysis) to real work at the company.
CONTEXT

The problem

Due Gooder grew by selling directly to students. Universities were a new channel, and a university can't adopt a tool that touches student data without a formal vendor review. UofL's process centered on the HECVAT (Higher Education Community Vendor Assessment Toolkit), the EDUCAUSE standard questionnaire that higher-ed security offices use to evaluate cloud vendors. Ours ran to roughly 900 questions across seven areas: organization, product, infrastructure, IT accessibility, case-specific use, AI, and privacy.

REQUIREMENTS → DESIGN

Translating requirements into controls

Most of the work was mapping each requirement area to how the system actually behaves, in language a security reviewer could verify. A sample of what we documented:

Requirement areaHow Due Gooder meets it
AuthenticationSingle sign-on through Google and Microsoft accounts (OpenID Connect). No passwords are stored by Due Gooder at all.
Data in transit & at restAll traffic encrypted with TLS; databases and backups encrypted at rest with AES-256.
Data locationAll production data and backups stay in U.S. data centers on managed cloud infrastructure.
Data ownership & exitThe institution owns its data, can request an export at any time, and institutional data is deleted at contract end.
Student privacyDue Gooder acts as a FERPA "school official" for institutional accounts. Product analytics use anonymous IDs instead of emails.
Responsible AIAI features run on a paid enterprise API that doesn't train on student data; any AI action that changes data needs the user's approval; AI use is logged and rate-limited.
AccessibilityADA compliance commitments documented for web and mobile apps.
A design change that came out of the review: our analytics originally identified users by email. To meet the privacy requirement, we switched to anonymized user IDs before launch. It was a small engineering change with a big trust payoff.
RISK MANAGEMENT

Handling the gaps honestly

As an early-stage startup, we didn't meet every control a large vendor would (for example, formal third-party audits). Rather than overstate anything, we answered "no" where that was the truth and described the compensating control and the plan to close the gap. That honesty is what made the review credible.

  • Transferring risk: we bought general liability, professional liability, and cyber/data-breach insurance, and sent proof of coverage to UofL's security office.
  • Contract risk: we worked through two rounds of redlines on the pilot agreement, covering liability, privacy obligations, governing law, and accessibility.
  • Reusability: the completed HECVAT is now a standard asset we can hand to the next university, which shortens every future review.

The full HECVAT is not published here because it documents our security architecture in detail.

OUTCOME & REFLECTION

Result

UofL approved Due Gooder for a campus-wide pilot covering the 2026–27 academic year, with the university sharing anonymized outcome data back to us. It became the anchor for our university channel. Due Gooder now has four signed university pilots.

What I learned

  • In enterprise sales, the security review is part of the product. Treating it as paperwork would have stalled the deal.
  • Writing for a technical reviewer means being specific and verifiable. "We take security seriously" earns nothing; "no passwords stored, SSO only" does.
  • Splitting the work by strength worked: Nate owned the architecture answers, and we worked through the rest together.
