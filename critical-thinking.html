Strategy Case: Adding a University Channel

This case uses the structured format from CIS 410 case analyses (problem, industry analysis, stakeholders, courses of action, impacts, and recommendation), applied to a decision my co-founder and I actually faced: should a consumer-first startup start selling to universities, and how?

Problem identificationPorter's Five ForcesStakeholder analysisDecision analysisBusiness writing

Due Gooder · Case written for this portfolio

Why this section uses Due Gooder work: I completed UofL's Sandbox program in place of CIS 320, 420, and 397, building Due Gooder full-time for course credit. This page applies the same methods those courses teach (systems analysis, project management, and case analysis) to real work at the company.

1. Problem

Due Gooder, an AI study platform, grew consumer-first. It launched in fall 2025 through organic social content and campus flyers, scaled through a creator program, and now has more than 320,000 student users. But consumer growth is seasonal (most new users arrive at the start of each semester), students are price-sensitive with low switching costs, and the students who choose a study app on their own tend to be the already motivated ones. That makes it hard to prove the product changes outcomes. The question: should Due Gooder add universities as a sales channel, and if so, how, without stalling the consumer engine that got it here?

2. Industry competitive analysis (Porter's Five Forces)

ForceStrengthAnalysis
RivalryHighCrowded student-productivity market: planner apps, AI study tools, and general tools like Notion all compete for the same students.
Threat of substitutesHighPaper planners, Google Calendar, and the calendar built into every learning management system are free and familiar.
Buyer power: studentsHighLow switching costs and strong price sensitivity. Students can leave at any time.
Buyer power: universitiesModerateLong procurement cycles and heavy requirements, but a deployed tool is sticky and renewals are likely if outcomes are shown.
Supplier powerModerateDependence on AI model providers and app stores, which set pricing and take a commission.
Barriers to entryLow (consumer) / High (institutional)AI makes a consumer app cheap to build. Passing university security reviews, integrations, and references is not, and that's a potential moat.

3. Stakeholders

  • Students: want a tool that works and keeps their data private; a school-provided license removes the cost barrier.
  • Universities (provost, student success, IT security): want measurable retention and success outcomes, at an acceptable level of risk.
  • Founders and team: limited time; every hour spent on enterprise sales is an hour not spent on the consumer engine.
  • Investors: want durable, less seasonal revenue and a defensible position.
  • Creators and agency partners: depend on continued consumer marketing investment.

4. Courses of action

  1. Stay consumer-only. Put all resources into creator marketing and the consumer subscription.
  2. Pivot fully to institutions. Stop consumer marketing and sell licenses to universities.
  3. Hybrid: add universities through low-cost pilots. Keep the consumer engine as the core; open a university channel with free or discounted pilots that convert to paid licenses once outcomes are shown.

5. Impacts

COA 1, consumer-only: the simplest option, and it keeps the team focused on what's working. But revenue stays seasonal, the selection-bias problem never gets solved, and the business stays exposed to low switching costs and a crowded field.

COA 2, full pivot: institutional contracts are larger and steadier, but sales cycles are long, and abandoning the consumer engine would throw away the asset that makes us credible to universities in the first place: students already using the product on their campus. Cash flow would suffer while the first contracts close.

COA 3, hybrid pilots: the consumer base becomes the sales pitch ("thousands of your students already use this"). Pilots generate outcome data across all students, not just self-selected ones, which addresses the selection-bias problem, and each completed security review becomes reusable. The cost is founder time and a slower path to institutional revenue.

6. Recommended course of action: COA 3, hybrid pilots

The hybrid approach keeps what works while building toward steadier revenue. An advisor with ed-tech leadership experience pushed us on the key point: universities pay for outcomes, and outcome data only becomes convincing when the tool is deployed broadly rather than adopted by the most motivated students. Pilots are how you get that data.

To control the main risk, the time split, the plan caps the number of concurrent pilots, uses a standard pilot agreement, and reuses the completed HECVAT so each new security review starts from finished work (see Section 01). The university channel runs on a documented, repeatable outreach system (see Section 02) instead of founder improvisation.

Early evidence: four signed university pilots, including a campus-wide pilot at the University of Louisville, while the consumer engine kept growing: the fall creator campaign drove 17.9 million views during the same period.

Rejecting COA 1 avoids leaving the business permanently seasonal. Rejecting COA 2 avoids betting the company on slow procurement cycles while giving up our strongest proof point.
